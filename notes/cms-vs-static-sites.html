Do company websites still need a CMS?

November 2026. This is an opinion piece.

I don't think most company websites need a CMS anymore. A static site with an integration that uses an LLM to write the pages is easier to maintain. That's true for developers, and I think it now holds for the people who don't write code too, who were the reason we installed a CMS in the first place.

Why we used a CMS

Not for technical reasons: static sites were always simpler to host and harder to break. We picked a CMS so that someone in marketing or HR could publish a news item or update a job posting without opening a ticket for a developer, because they had no other way to edit the website.

What that interface costs

A server, a database, backups, core updates, plugin updates, security patches, and a theme that breaks whenever one of those updates goes wrong. Someone has to own all of that for as long as the site lives, and on a typical company site that someone is a developer.

Editors pay too. They have to learn an admin panel, remember which block to use, keep images at the right size and avoid breaking the layout. In my experience most of them open it a few times a year, forget how it works in between, and end up asking a developer anyway.

What changed

An LLM can now take a request in plain language ("add a news item about the new office, here are three photos and the address") and turn it into a page that follows the site's templates and style. Editors don't need an interface to the website anymore. Describing the change is enough.

The flow I have in mind:

  1. someone describes the page they want, in a chat, a form or an email;
  2. the LLM writes the page in the site's format and opens a pull request;
  3. the change gets a preview link, and the person who asked checks it there;
  4. a merge publishes it, and the static site is rebuilt and deployed.

Step 3 replaces the approval workflow of a CMS, and I'd argue it does the job better. Every change is versioned, can be reviewed, and can be reverted with one click, and nothing reaches the live site until someone approves it.

All that's left to maintain is a set of HTML or Markdown files, a template and a deploy pipeline. This site is built exactly that way, and hosting it costs nothing.

Where I would still use a CMS

When:

  • many editors publish every day and need roles, permissions and an approval chain;
  • content changes several times an hour, as on a news site;
  • business teams manage structured data, such as a product catalog, rather than pages.

Most company websites are none of these. They have a few dozen pages, a news section updated a few times a month and a careers page, and for them the CMS is mostly overhead.

What to watch out for

What the LLM produces is a draft. Someone has to read it before it goes live, especially claims about the company, numbers, legal wording and anything about clients. The pull request makes that review quick, but someone still has to do it.

The integration needs an owner as well. It's far less work than keeping a CMS patched, but templates evolve, models change, and the prompts that describe the site's style need updating. You do that work when you decide to improve the site, instead of whenever a plugin ships a security fix.
